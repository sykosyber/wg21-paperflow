The same paper scores a little differently each time

The AI has some randomness, so three runs of one paper give three slightly different totals. Usually that doesn't matter. When a paper sits near a band edge, it can tip it into a different band.

Three runs of five papers, total out of 14

all 3 runs in one bandruns land in two bands

How to read it: each dot is one run. The shaded zones are paperweight's bands. P2040R0 scored 8.00 (Heavy) once and 6.33 and 6.67 (Medium) twice. Across all papers, repeat runs agreed on the band 20 of 24 times. paperweight's own notes recorded 9 of 10 on the model it was tuned on.

Real run

Sometimes it won't grade a paper at all

Before grading, the AI decides whether the paper is a proposal. For two papers it changed its mind between runs, so the same paper sometimes got a score and sometimes got nothing.

What changed: every report now flags a paper like this and leaves it out of the accuracy numbers, with the reason. P4020R0, a position paper, was "not a proposal" all 3 times, which is a fair call.

Proxy study

How we tested the harness's own maths

The harness's job is to say whether a rulebook change made scores better, worse or no real change. To check that verdict, we needed cases where we already know the right answer. Real human reviews give us those.

Every ICLR 2025 paper has several human reviewers

"Old version"One reviewer's score, standing in for paperweight before a change.
"New version"The average of two reviewers. It lands 19% closer to the panel, so it really is better.
Answer keyThe average of the remaining reviewers, standing in for the human grades.
pick 10 random papers→ask the harness: better, worse or no change?→repeat 200 times→count the answers

Why this works: we know the new version is better, so the right answer is always "better". We then do the same with 20, 40 and 80 papers. "Error" here means how many points a score misses the answer key by, on average.

6
Proxy study

The old check cried wolf, so we replaced it

Now the opposite test. "Old" is one reviewer and "new" is a different reviewer: neither is better, so the right answer is always "no real change". Any "better" or "worse" is a false alarm.

200 tries on 10 papers

false alarmthe allowance: 5%, or 10 of 200

Where 5% comes from: any check that separates real changes from luck is allowed to be fooled sometimes. The standard in science, and the setting the spec uses, is at most 5% of the time: 1 in 20. The old check went over that allowance on 10 papers. The new one stays inside it.

How each check decides

Old: bootstrap interval

  1. Treat the 10 papers as the whole world.
  2. Draw 10 of them at random, allowing repeats, and average the old-vs-new difference.
  3. Do that 2,000 times and keep the middle 95%.
  4. If zero is outside that range, call it a real change.
The flaw: redraws of the same 10 papers look too alike, so the range comes out too narrow.

New: permutation test

  1. If nothing changed, "old" and "new" are interchangeable on each paper.
  2. Try every way of swapping them: 1,024 ways for 10 papers.
  3. That shows every result pure luck could give.
  4. Call it real only if the actual result beats 95% of them.
It makes no assumptions, so it keeps the 5% promise.

The trade-off: the new check is more careful, so it also says "better" less often. That is part of why step 5's numbers are low on 10 papers. The old check looked more sensitive only because it said "better" too easily.
